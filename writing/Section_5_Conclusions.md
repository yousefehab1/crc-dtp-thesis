# 5. Conclusions and Future Directions

## 5.1 Conclusions

This thesis asked whether a drug-tolerant persister signature derived from colorectal cancer cell lines carries clinical prognostic value in colorectal cancer patients, and then explored, without expecting a particular answer, whether that value extends to other tissues and to metastasis. The work supports a clear and bounded conclusion.

The main conclusion is that it does have clinical value in colorectal cancer. The up-regulated persister score is a genuine prognostic signal, and the signal is its own rather than a restatement of established biology: it replicated across two cohorts and two platforms, was clearest for recurrence and strongest in the adjuvant-treated subset, and survived adjustment for stage, microsatellite status and both molecular-subtype taxonomies. It sat specifically on the persister and fetal-regenerative axis rather than on generic stemness or proliferation, and it converges with an independently derived slow-cycling colorectal subtype that also marks poor outcome. A cell-line-derived signature therefore translates to independent prognostic information in patients, which was the primary aim of the work. One observation sharpens the interpretation: the score is prognostic even in TCGA tumours that were profiled before any chemotherapy, which means the persister state it measures is not created by treatment alone but is already present in aggressive, treatment-naive tumours, presumably induced by intrinsic microenvironmental stress, and is then selected for by chemotherapy.

The two exploratory questions returned weaker answers, and it is important to state them as such rather than over-claim. The signature is not a universal pan-cancer marker: its prognostic value was weak overall and concentrated in a few tissues, notably low-grade glioma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, which is unsurprising for a signature selected under one drug in one cancer type. And it was not enriched in bulk metastasis; because a bulk score cannot resolve a state confined to a minority of cells, this is best read as an unresolved question requiring higher-resolution methods rather than a true absence. The bounded conclusion, then, is that the primary aim is met, the signature has independent prognostic value in colorectal cancer, while its behaviour in other tissues and in metastasis is left, honestly, as exploratory.

## 5.2 Future directions

The most direct next step is to test the signature where it should be strongest: in patients sampled before and after chemotherapy. A cohort with paired pre- and post-treatment tumour material, such as the patient-derived xenograft series recently established by the laboratory, would allow the induction of the persister signature by treatment to be measured directly in the same tumour, rather than inferred from a treated-versus-untreated comparison across patients. This is the cleanest test of the core biology and the natural complement to external validation in an independent, uniformly treated colorectal cohort with recorded recurrence. For clinical use, the signature would not require whole-transcriptome profiling: it could be locked onto a targeted panel, for example a NanoString or quantitative PCR readout of its member genes, with a fixed, pre-registered scoring threshold, making it deployable on routine formalin-fixed clinical material.

The bulk-tissue limitation should be addressed with single-cell resolution. Single-cell RNA sequencing of colorectal tumours would show directly which cell populations carry the persister signature, in particular whether it is induced in the malignant epithelial compartment or reflects the stromal and mesenchymal content of aggressive tumours, and would resolve the metastasis question that a bulk paired design could not: whether the programme is present in a rare subpopulation of matched primary and metastatic tumours, and whether the down, revival and regenerative programmes that moved in the bulk analysis reflect distinct cell types. This single-cell view is the most important methodological upgrade for the next phase of the work.

The tissue-specific pan-cancer leads are worth pursuing directly rather than treating as incidental. The strength of the signal in low-grade glioma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma is a concrete, testable hypothesis: that the persister and fetal-regenerative programme marks the plasticity-driven aggressive fraction of these tumours. Testing it would mean examining the score against treatment and outcome within these tissues specifically, ideally with the same independence checks used here, and separating a genuine persister effect from the wide prognostic spread that inflates discrimination in low-grade glioma.

Two smaller methodological checks remain before the analysis is finalised: confirming that the pathway-derived subtype classifier receives expression values on its intended scale, and settling the multiple-testing correction family. As set out in the Discussion, both are deliberate methodological choices rather than weaknesses, and neither affects the main findings, which hold under conservative correction.

Finally, the persister interpretation invites experimental follow-up beyond association. The signal was defined from a colorectal persister model, and the natural test of the hypothesis is functional: whether tumours or patient-derived models scoring high for the programme are in fact enriched for drug-tolerant, slow-cycling cells under chemotherapy, and whether targeting the state changes the likelihood of relapse. Such work would move the programme from a prognostic marker, which this thesis supports it to be in colorectal cancer, toward a causal account of the relapse it is intended to explain.
