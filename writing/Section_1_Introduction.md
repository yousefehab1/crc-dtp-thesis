# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Colorectal cancer and the problem of relapse

Colorectal cancer is among the most commonly diagnosed cancers worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer death (Sung et al. 2021). For patients with stage II and stage III disease, surgery is followed in most cases by adjuvant chemotherapy, which has been built around 5-fluorouracil since the 1990s and, for higher-risk and node-positive disease, is now most often given as a 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin combination (André et al. 2004; Dekker et al. 2019). Adjuvant treatment lowers the risk of recurrence and improves survival, and it is the standard of care for node-positive disease and for higher-risk node-negative disease (André et al. 2004).

The unsolved problem is that a substantial minority of treated patients still relapse (Dekker et al. 2019). Recurrence after apparently curative surgery and a full course of cytotoxic therapy is the event that most often precedes death from colorectal cancer, and it can occur even when the primary tumour has responded and no residual disease is detectable by standard means. This pattern, in which a treatment shrinks the bulk of a tumour yet fails to prevent later regrowth, is difficult to explain by acquired resistance mutations alone, because relapse can arise quickly and from cells that were, by genetic criteria, sensitive to the drug (Marine et al. 2020). Genetic and non-genetic mechanisms are not mutually exclusive here, but complementary: a non-genetic, reversible tolerance can let a subset of cells survive the initial cytotoxic insult, and this surviving reservoir then provides the viable cells within which stable, genetically encoded resistance can subsequently arise (Sharma et al. 2010; Rehman et al. 2021; Marine et al. 2020). It is this early, transient, non-genetic tolerance, rather than the later mutations, that a transcriptional signature could hope to capture in a patient's tumour. A transcriptional state that marks such cells, if it could be measured in a patient's tumour, would connect the biology of treatment survival to the clinical event, relapse, that adjuvant therapy is meant to prevent. That connection is the motivation for this thesis.

## 1.2 The drug-tolerant persister state

The drug-tolerant persister (DTP) state is a candidate for such a population. It describes a reversible, non-mutational form of tolerance in which tumour cells enter a slow-cycling, drug-tolerant phenotype under cytotoxic or targeted pressure, survive while most of the tumour is killed, and can exit the state and resume proliferation once the pressure is removed (Sharma et al. 2010). The defining features are that the state is reversible and is not caused by a fixed genetic change: cells that have passed through it are, once re-expanded, as sensitive to the drug as the original population, which distinguishes persistence from classical acquired resistance (Sharma et al. 2010; Marine et al. 2020). Because entry into and exit from the state are governed by transcriptional and chromatin-level regulation rather than by mutation, the persister phenotype is fundamentally a cell-state property (Sharma et al. 2010; Marine et al. 2020).

The concept was first described in targeted-therapy settings, where a small fraction of cancer cells survived otherwise lethal drug exposure and formed a reservoir from which resistant colonies later emerged (Sharma et al. 2010), and it has since been generalised across many cancer types and drug classes as a common early step on the path to resistance (Marine et al. 2020). In colorectal cancer treated with 5-fluorouracil-based chemotherapy, an analogous treatment-surviving state has been characterised in patient-derived models, where it resembles embryonic diapause and where the capacity to enter it appears to be broadly shared across the tumour cell population rather than confined to a rare pre-existing clone (Rehman et al. 2021). It is from a colorectal persister model of this kind that the signature used in this thesis was derived (Atlasi Laboratory, unpublished; Section 1.4).

One conceptual point should be planted here because it recurs throughout the thesis. Whether the capacity to persist is restricted to a rare subpopulation (Sharma et al. 2010) or is broadly available to tumour cells (Rehman et al. 2021), at any one moment only a fraction of cells actually occupy the persister state. This is what makes a transcriptional signature the natural way to look for it, because a signature can be scored in any sample where the relevant genes are measured. It is also the source of the main caveat on the approach used here: a measurement taken from bulk tissue averages over all the cells present, so a state occupied by only a fraction of cells will be diluted in a bulk score. This tension between a cell-state biology and a bulk measurement is returned to in the Discussion.

## 1.3 Transcriptomic signatures and single-sample scoring

A biological state such as persistence is better captured by the coordinated behaviour of a set of genes than by any single marker. No one gene reports the state cleanly, whereas the joint up- and down-regulation of many genes is both more specific to the state and more robust to the noise of any individual measurement (Subramanian et al. 2005). A signature-based approach is therefore less sensitive to platform differences and to the dropout of any single gene, because the score draws on the whole set rather than on one transcript. This robustness is what allows the same programme to be measured on a microarray in one cohort and on RNA sequencing in another and still be compared.

The signatures in this thesis were scored with single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), a rank-based method that quantifies, within one sample, whether the genes of a set are coordinately high relative to the rest of the transcriptome of that same sample (Barbie et al. 2009), as implemented in the gene set variation analysis framework (Hänzelmann et al. 2013). The method has two properties that matter for the design used here. First, it is computed per sample, so every patient receives a continuous score that can be entered directly into a survival model, rather than a group-level statistic (Barbie et al. 2009). Second, the score depends only on the within-sample ranking of genes, so it is invariant to any monotonic transformation of the expression values, and once each platform is placed on a consistent scale the scores are comparable across microarray and RNA-sequencing data (Hänzelmann et al. 2013). The methodological detail of the scoring is provided in the Materials and Methods. The relevant principle is that a per-sample rank statistic converts a gene set into a patient-level covariate that remains comparable across platforms.

Using a continuous score, rather than dividing patients into signature-positive and signature-negative groups, avoids the loss of information and the arbitrariness of a threshold, and it preserves statistical power. Survival was analysed at a fixed landmark, which converts a time-to-event outcome into a clean classification at a defined horizon and so allows the same three-year question to be asked consistently across cohorts. Together these choices make the score a single, interpretable, per-patient quantity that can be tested the same way in every dataset.

The strength of the design is that it does not rest on any one association. A single significant result in a single cohort is weak evidence, because it can arise from chance, from a cohort-specific artefact, or from a confounder peculiar to that dataset. The approach taken here instead tests the same programme in independent cohorts, on independent platforms, and against different biological questions, so that agreement across these lines of evidence, rather than significance in any one of them, is what counts. This triangulation across datasets is the backbone of the thesis and sets up the three-part structure of the analysis described in Section 1.6.

Two limits of the approach should be stated plainly at the outset. A bulk single-sample score measures an average over all the cells in a sample, so a state occupied by only a fraction of cells is measured only as its diluted contribution to the whole. And because ssGSEA scores are relative within each sample, consistent normalisation across samples and platforms is essential for the scores to be comparable (Hänzelmann et al. 2013). Both points are acknowledged here and are taken up again in the Discussion.

## 1.4 The gene sets and why they were chosen

The analysis rests on a panel of gene sets, summarised in Table 1.1. The panel has two parts: the persister signature that is the object of the study, and a set of reference programmes chosen so that the specificity of any persister result can be judged.

The persister programme itself is defined by two gene sets, an up-regulated component (Up) and a down-regulated component (Down), which together describe the transcriptional state that cells enter under cytotoxic pressure. A Composite score is defined as Up minus Down, which sharpens the contrast by combining the two directions into a single quantity, so that a cell strongly in the persister state scores high on the up-regulated genes and low on the down-regulated genes at once. These Up and Down sets are a laboratory-derived signature (Atlasi Laboratory; Mirzadeh Azad et al., in press). They were defined from two colorectal cancer cell lines of contrasting genetic background, HCT116 (microsatellite-instable, TP53/APC wild-type) and SW620 (microsatellite-stable, TP53/APC mutant), each exposed to a sub-lethal dose of 5-fluorouracil (5 to 10 uM) for 14 days to induce a drug-tolerant persister state that is slow-cycling and reverts to the parental state once the drug is withdrawn. The Up and Down sets are the genes consistently up- and down-regulated in the persister cells across both models, so they capture a drug-tolerance programme shared by two genetically distinct colorectal backgrounds rather than a line-specific response. The Up set contains 313 genes and the Down set 398 genes. Of note, in the source study this signature was itself most enriched in the CMS4 and PDS2 tumour subtypes, the mesenchymal, slow-cycling, revival-stem-like classes with the poorest clinical outcome, which is the same biology this thesis independently finds the signature to track (Section 3.3).

The six reference signatures are not incidental to the design; they are central to it. A persister state does not sit in isolation but overlaps biologically with stemness, regeneration and proliferation (Marine et al. 2020; Rehman et al. 2021), and a prognostic signal that proved to be no more than a restatement of generic stemness or of proliferation would be far less informative than one specific to the persister programme. The reference sets were therefore chosen to span exactly the axes a sceptic would raise, so that the persister result could be tested against each of them. The fetal intestinal programme captures the fetal-like, regenerative reprogramming that injured and malignant intestinal epithelium re-enters, which is the closest biological neighbour to a persister state (Mustata et al. 2013; Yui et al. 2018). The revival stem cell programme (revSC) captures the transient, Lgr5-negative, Clusterin-marked regenerative population that appears after epithelial injury (Ayyaz et al. 2019), and the regenerative stem cell programme (RSC) captures a related adult regenerative state (Vasquez et al. 2022). These revival and regenerative populations carry exactly the features that make them the persister state's nearest neighbours: they are slow-cycling rather than proliferative, they are driven by YAP and TEAD signalling, and they have an inflammatory transcriptional character. Together with the fetal set they represent the regeneration axis, the closest biological neighbour to a drug-tolerant persister state. The crypt-base columnar cell programme (CBC) captures canonical Lgr5-type intestinal stemness (Vasquez et al. 2022), the obvious stemness control. The inflammatory bowel disease epithelial programme (IBD) captures an inflammation-driven regenerative state, defined from the epithelial inflamed-versus-healthy contrast so that it reflects epithelial regeneration rather than immune infiltration (Smillie et al. 2019). Finally, the MYC target module captures proliferation, the most obvious alternative explanation for any association with outcome (Liberzon et al. 2015).

The reasoning that this panel makes possible is the heart of the specificity argument. If the persister up score tracks patient outcome while these neighbouring programmes largely do not, then the signal is specific to the persister and fetal-regenerative axis rather than to stemness or proliferation in general. That is exactly the comparison reported in Section 3.2, where the reference sets are run through the identical tests. One methodological point belongs here: a gene set is scored only where a sufficient fraction of its genes is present in a given expression matrix, under the coverage gate described in the Methods, which keeps the scores comparable across platforms.

## 1.5 Molecular subtypes as confounders and effect modifiers

Colorectal cancer is not a single disease, and several established taxonomies capture its heterogeneity. The consensus molecular subtypes (CMS) divide the disease into four transcriptional classes with distinct biology and prognosis (Guinney et al. 2015). CMS1 tumours are microsatellite-instable and immune-infiltrated; CMS2 tumours are epithelial and driven by canonical WNT and MYC activation; CMS3 tumours show metabolic dysregulation; and CMS4 tumours are mesenchymal, marked by transforming growth factor beta activation, stromal invasion and the poorest prognosis. The pathway-derived subtypes (PDS) provide a complementary classification based on the activity of core signalling pathways (Malla et al. 2024): PDS1 tumours are canonical, LGR5-positive stem-rich and proliferative with a good prognosis; PDS2 tumours are regenerative and ANXA1-positive, with elevated stromal and immune content; and PDS3 tumours form a slow-cycling, differentiated subset that carries the worst prognosis in locally advanced disease. Microsatellite status is a further axis: microsatellite-instable (MSI) tumours are mismatch-repair deficient, hypermutated and immune-rich, and generally carry a better early-stage prognosis, whereas microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumours make up the majority and are chromosomally unstable. Tumour stage carries independent prognostic weight and is used routinely in the clinic (Dekker et al. 2019). These subtypes are relevant to the present work because the persister and regenerative biology a DTP score measures is concentrated in particular classes, notably the mesenchymal CMS4 and the regenerative PDS2, so the score is expected to intersect with, and must be distinguished from, the established taxonomy. These classifications are therefore introduced not for their own sake but because of the methodological problem they pose for this thesis. Each of them is correlated with the stemness, regeneration and proliferation biology that a persister signature also touches, so a score that appeared prognostic might in fact be re-expressing subtype, stage or microsatellite status rather than adding information of its own. Any claim that the persister score is prognostic must therefore be shown to survive adjustment for these established variables, and to be tested for whether its effect merely differs across their subgroups. This is the motivation for the score-across-subtype analysis of Section 3.3 and for the adjusted and interaction models of Section 3.4.

## 1.6 Gap, hypothesis and aims

The gap this thesis addresses is a translational one. The drug-tolerant persister programme was defined in colorectal cancer cell lines exposed to chemotherapy (Section 1.4), and it is not known whether a signature derived from such an in vitro model carries any clinical information in patient tumours. The central question is therefore direct: does the cell-line-derived persister signature have prognostic value in colorectal cancer patients?

The primary hypothesis is that it does. A tumour scoring high for the persister programme is proposed to be in a more aggressive, treatment-tolerant biological state, and so to predict worse survival and earlier recurrence, and this prognostic information is proposed to be its own rather than a restatement of stage, microsatellite status or molecular subtype. The primary aim of the thesis is to test this in two independent colorectal cohorts and to establish whether the signal is independent of the standard clinicopathological and molecular variables. This is the core of the work.

Two further questions are then explored, but as secondary and deliberately exploratory extensions rather than as necessary consequences of the hypothesis. The first is whether the same signature carries prognostic value in other cancer types. There is no strong biological reason to expect that it should: the signature was selected under 5-fluorouracil, and different cancers are driven by different biology and treated with different drugs, so a 5-FU response programme need not predict outcome in, for example, tamoxifen-treated breast cancer or a leukaemia treated with a hypomethylating agent. This strand is therefore descriptive, asking where, if anywhere, the signal recurs, not testing a prediction that it must. The second is whether the programme is enriched in metastatic tissue. Here the expectation should also be modest, because persister cells are by definition dormant and slow-cycling whereas metastasis is driven by disseminating, proliferating cells; a persister programme might contribute only to the earliest, seeding phase of metastasis and be absent from established lesions. This strand is likewise exploratory and its result is read with that caveat.

The three aims therefore map onto three analytical modules of decreasing centrality: to establish the clinical prognostic value of the persister signature in primary colorectal cancer and its independence from established variables (the primary aim); to explore whether that value extends to other solid tumours of The Cancer Genome Atlas (Liu et al. 2018); and to explore whether the programme is enriched in matched metastatic tissue. All three use the shared single-sample scoring approach of Section 1.3 and the reference panel of Section 1.4, so that any prognostic signal can be judged for specificity against neighbouring stemness, regeneration and proliferation biology. The overall design is shown as the study-design schematic at the start of the Results.
